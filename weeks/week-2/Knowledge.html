In plain words · "context" and "the window"

Context is everything the model can read while producing its next reply: your messages, its own replies, file contents it has opened, command output, and the standing instructions loaded at start-up. Not your repo, not its training data, not yesterday's session: just this text.

The window is the hard limit on how much of that text fits: a fixed budget of tokens (word-pieces, roughly three-quarters of an English word each; code runs denser). When the window is full, something must be dropped or squashed before anything new fits. Every session you'll ever run lives inside that budget.

Act 1 · Day 1 · ~50 min

The window: what the model actually sees

Act 1 has one idea: the model's entire world is a single block of text with a hard size limit, and you are its editor. Everything competes for that space: the tool's own instructions, your wiring from week 1, your standing rules, your questions, every file it reads, every paste you drop in. Space is the first constraint. Attention is the second: the fuller the window, the worse the model uses any one part of it. And the meter from week 1 is the third: every token in the window is work the system does again and again, all session long.

Four movements: see the window's parts and their sizes (M1) · learn where attention goes as it fills (M2) · connect window discipline to your Max limits (M3) · then open /context on your own machine and read your real numbers (Hands-on 01).

In plain words · "attention" without the maths

When the model answers, it doesn't read the window the way you read a page, start to finish with equal care. It weighs every piece of text against every other piece and decides what matters for the next word. That weighing is attention. It's a budget: more text in the window means the weighing is spread thinner, so any single fact gets less of it. A sharp question in a lean window gets concentrated attention; the same question after two pasted logs is fighting 40,000 other tokens for notice.

Keep thisOne block of text, three costs: space fills, attention thins, spend compounds per turn. Every context decision trades against those three.

Under the hood · why connected servers cost tokens while idle

The model can only call a tool it can see. So every session, Claude Code writes the full catalogue of available tools into the window as text: name, description, parameters, for every tool of every connected MCP server. A server with 30 tools ships 30 schemas, every session, whether you call one of them or none. That's why the meter charges you at "connected", not at "used". The fix isn't to unwire your week 1 work; it's scope: a server used by one project belongs in that project's config, not in your user-level config where it taxes every session on the machine.

Did you know · the window is why "it forgot" is usually "it never saw"

Sessions don't share memory. Yesterday's session was a different window that no longer exists anywhere. When today's Claude "forgets" your date format, it isn't forgetting: that instruction was never in today's window. The only things every session sees are the standing layers, which is exactly why this week puts your rules there.

Act 1 · Movement M2

A fuller window isn't just smaller. It's dumber about its middle.

200k tokens of capacity does not mean 200k tokens of equal recall. Research on long-context models keeps finding the same shape: content at the start and the end of the window is used well, and content buried in the middle is used worst. As a session grows, everything you said two hours ago migrates toward that middle. The practical name for the slow decay you feel in hour four is context rot: the window still contains the fact; the model just weights it less than the 40,000 fresher tokens sitting on top of it.

How industry does it · "lost in the middle" is a named result

The middle sag isn't cohort folklore. Liu et al. published "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts" in 2023, showing retrieval accuracy dropping sharply for facts placed mid-context across several long-context models, with the ends holding up. Model vendors have chipped at it since, and newer models sag less, but the operational advice hasn't changed: put load-bearing constraints where position is guaranteed, and keep the window lean so there is less middle to get lost in.

By the way · headroom: don't start big work at 80%

A corollary worth a habit: check the window before starting anything substantial. Kicking off a refactor at 80% full means the plan, the diffs and the test output all land in the wobbliest part of a nearly full window, and an automatic squeeze is coming mid-task. Big work starts in fresh or freshly compacted sessions. The /context command you meet in Hands-on 01 is how you check; "start heavy work above 60%? no" is a rule worth writing down.

Act 1 · Movement M3

The window travels with every turn. That's the bill.

Here's the mechanism week 1's meters were hinting at. The model is stateless between turns: each time it answers, the system hands it the conversation again and it reads the window afresh. Ask 30 questions in a 100k-token session and the system has processed something like 30 windows' worth of text, not one. Caching softens this: the unchanged start of the window is reprocessed at a steep discount. But nothing makes a fat window free, and on Max the observable consequence is blunt: dirty, long sessions drain your weekly limits several times faster than clean ones doing the same work.

Under the hood · what the cache actually caches

Serving systems cache the internal computation for a prefix: the window's unchanged opening run of tokens. If turn 12 arrives and the first 90k tokens are byte-identical to turn 11's first 90k, that stretch is a cheap cache read, and only the new tail is processed at full price. Anything that rewrites early content (editing an old message, reordering context) changes the prefix, and everything after the change point is full price again. Claude Code manages this for you; your lever is behavioural: sessions that accumulate forward keep the discount, sessions that thrash don't.

Keep thisWindow linear, ledger compounding: every token of standing junk is re-paid each turn. Lean starts are the cheapest optimisation you own.

Act 2 · Movement M5

A rule earns its place or it poisons the file

The failure mode of CLAUDE.md is not absence; it's the 200-line manifesto. Aspirational prose ("write clean code") spends attention and changes nothing. Stale rules are worse: one instruction that contradicts the code teaches the model to distrust the whole file. The anatomy of a rule that works has three parts: a trigger (when it applies), an imperative (do this, never that), and a check (a way to tell it was followed). "Run node --test before claiming done" has all three. "Prioritise quality" has none.

The template ships at weeks/week-2/data/handoff-template.md; here is what each section is for, and the failure it prevents:

SectionHoldsPrevents
StateDone and verified · half-done · untouched, one line eachRe-verifying finished work; trusting unfinished work
DecisionsChoices made this session, each with its one-line reasonTomorrow relitigating today (the ADR failure, at day scale)
Next stepThe single next action, cold-startable: file, command, intentThe 25-minute "where was I" ramp
GotchasThe trap that already cost you time once todayPaying for the same discovery twice
VerifyCommands that prove the state still holdsBuilding on a base that shifted overnight

The quality bar is transferability: not "will this remind me" but "could a session with no memory of today continue from this page alone". Every session is that stranger. And note the boundary with Act 4: a decision that should outlive the week graduates from the handoff's Decisions section into an ADR; the handoff is the working set, not the archive.

By the way · handoffs compound beyond day boundaries

The same file works whenever a window must die mid-stream: before a risky /clear, when switching machines, when a session has degraded past compacting, or when handing work to an actual colleague and their session. Once the habit exists, "this session is past saving" stops being a loss: two minutes of handoff and the work continues elsewhere, clean.

What you decide: your 6pm ritual. The programme's: handoff written by the session, corrected by you, committed if the repo is yours: then /clear or close without guilt. Keep thisEnd days by authoring, not abandoning. The handoff is two minutes that buys tomorrow's first half-hour.
