In plain words · "context" and "the window"

Context is everything the model can read while producing its next reply. That means your messages, its own replies, file contents it has opened, command output, and the standing instructions loaded at start-up. It does not mean your repo, its training data or yesterday's session. It is just this text.

The window is the hard limit on how much of that text fits. It is a fixed budget of tokens. A token is a word-piece, roughly three-quarters of an English word, and code runs denser. When the window is full, something must be dropped or squashed before anything new fits. Every session you'll ever run lives inside that budget.

What is · the session you ran last month

You re-explain your codebase every single day

Monday: you tell Claude the test command, the date format, the module that must never be edited by hand. It does good work. Tuesday: new session, blank window, and you type it all again. Or you don't, and it guesses wrong.

By Thursday the session is four hours old and sluggish. You pasted two logs and a schema dump somewhere around lunch. Now it's confusing the old error with the new one, and calling functions that stopped existing an hour ago.

None of that is the model being dumb.

  • Old pastes never leave; they sit in the window and mislead.
  • The instructions you gave yesterday aren't "known". They were text in a window that no longer exists.

You've been running a system whose working memory you never looked at. You've paid for that in repeated typing and derailed afternoons.

Act 1 · Day 1 · ~50 min

The window: what the model actually sees

Act 1 has one idea: the model's entire world is a single block of text with a hard size limit, and you are that block's editor.

Everything competes for the space. That includes the tool's own instructions, your wiring from week 1, and your standing rules. It also includes your questions, every file it reads, and every paste you drop in.

Three costs follow. Space is the first: the block fills. Attention is the second: the fuller the window, the worse the model uses any one part of it. Spend is the third, the meter from week 1: every token in the window is work the system does again on every turn, all session long.

  • M1 shows the window's parts and their sizes.
  • M2 shows where attention goes as the window fills.
  • M3 connects window discipline to your Max limits.
  • Hands-on 01 has you open /context on your own machine and read your real numbers.
In plain words · "attention" without the maths

When the model answers, it doesn't read the window the way you read a page, start to finish with equal care. It weighs every piece of text against every other piece and decides what matters for the next word. That weighing is attention.

Attention is a budget. More text in the window means the weighing is spread thinner, so any single fact gets less of it. A sharp question in a lean window gets concentrated attention; the same question after two pasted logs is fighting 40,000 other tokens for notice.

M1 · Window anatomy

Your window is never empty, even before you type

GoalKnow the window's standing occupants and their rough sizes, so a full window is a diagnosis, never a mystery.

A fresh Claude Code session looks blank. It isn't. Before your first word, the window already carries four things.

  • It carries the system prompt, which is the tool's own standing instructions.
  • It carries the definitions of every built-in tool.
  • It carries the schemas of every MCP server you wired last week.
  • It carries every CLAUDE.md your scopes load.

These occupants cost window space in every session, and until now you've never itemised that cost.

The meter below is a 200,000-token window, the standard size you work in. The sizes are illustrative shapes, rounded from real sessions; Hands-on 01 replaces them with your machine's true numbers.

Two families live in the window.

  • Standing context loads before you type: the system prompt, tool definitions, MCP schemas, and CLAUDE.md files. You engineer it once and it rides every session.
  • Session context accumulates as you work: your messages, file reads, command output, and pastes. You manage it hour by hour.

Under the hood · why connected servers cost tokens while idle

The model can only call a tool it can see. So every session, Claude Code writes the full catalogue of available tools into the window as text. That means the name, description and parameters, for every tool of every connected MCP server. A server with 30 tools ships 30 schemas, every session, whether you call one of them or none. That's why the meter charges you at "connected", not at "used".

Don't unwire your week 1 work; move it to the right scope instead. A server used by one project belongs in that project's config. In your user-level config it taxes every session on the machine.

Did you know · "it forgot" is usually "it never saw"

Sessions don't share memory. Yesterday's session was a different window that no longer exists anywhere. When today's Claude "forgets" your date format, it isn't forgetting: that instruction was never in today's window. The only things every session sees are the standing layers, which is exactly why this week puts your rules there.

M2 · The attention budget

As the window fills, the model gets worse at using its middle

GoalFeel what position does to a load-bearing fact, so you place facts where they hold.

200k tokens of capacity does not mean 200k tokens of equal recall.

  • Research on long-context models keeps finding the same shape. Content at the start and the end of the window is used well. Content buried in the middle is used worst.
  • As a session grows, everything you said two hours ago migrates toward that middle.
  • The slow decay you feel in hour four has a name: context rot. The window still contains the fact; the model just weights it less than the 40,000 fresher tokens sitting on top of it.

The test below places one real rule at three positions in a long, busy session. The rule comes from this week's gym: "the archive is append-only; never dedupe it in place". It's a model of the published long-context results rather than a live model call. The point is the shape. You'll feel the real thing the first time a four-hour session ignores something you said at 9am.

Widget · the position test · one rule, three placements

Pick a placement.

The bar is the window, start on the left. Place the rule.

How industry does it · "lost in the middle" is a named result

The middle sag isn't cohort folklore. Liu et al. published "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts" in 2023. It showed retrieval accuracy dropping sharply for facts placed mid-context across several long-context models, with the ends holding up.

Model vendors have chipped at it since, and newer models sag less. The practical advice hasn't changed. Put load-bearing constraints where position is guaranteed. Keep the window lean, so there is less middle to get lost in.

By the way · headroom: don't start big work at 80%

One habit follows from this. Check the window before starting anything substantial. Start a refactor at 80% full, and the plan, the diffs and the test output all land in the weakest part of the window. An automatic squeeze is coming mid-task too.

Big work starts in fresh or freshly compacted sessions. The /context command you meet in Hands-on 01 is how you check; "start heavy work above 60%? no" is a rule worth writing down.

What you decide: the three facts about your repo that must never depend on position: your first CLAUDE.md rules, one act from now.
M3 · Context is spend

The whole window is re-sent and re-read on every turn. That's the bill.

GoalSee how window size compounds across turns, and what keeps the cache discount alive.

Here's the mechanism week 1's meters were hinting at.

  • The model is stateless between turns: each time it answers, the system hands it the conversation again and it reads the window afresh.
  • Ask 30 questions in a 100k-token session and the system has processed something like 30 windows' worth of text, not one.
  • Caching softens this: the unchanged start of the window is reprocessed at a steep discount. But nothing makes a fat window free.

The ledger below models an afternoon. Choose how much standing-plus-junk context the session carries and how many turns you run.

  • The window is what you see in /context: linear, bounded, calm-looking.
  • The ledger is what the system processed. Every turn re-reads everything before it, so the ledger compounds.
  • The toggle models the cache: append-only sessions keep the discount, and churned early context breaks it.
  • Units are abstract on purpose. Max shows you no token bills, only limits that run out, so the ratio is the lesson.

Under the hood · what the cache actually caches

Serving systems cache the internal computation for a prefix: the window's unchanged opening run of tokens. If turn 12 arrives and the first 90k tokens are byte-identical to turn 11's first 90k, that stretch is a cheap cache read. Only the new tail is processed at full price.

Anything that rewrites early content (editing an old message, reordering context) changes the prefix, and everything after the change point is full price again. Claude Code manages this for you. Your lever is behavioural: sessions that accumulate forward keep the discount, and sessions that thrash don't.

Act 2 · Day 2 · ~35 min

CLAUDE.md: the always-on spec

One file changes the physics of Act 1. CLAUDE.md is a markdown file Claude Code loads into the window at the start of every session, before your first word.

Put a rule in it and the rule holds tomorrow, and next week. It holds in the session a teammate opens from the same repo. It's the only place where "I told it once" is actually true.

The catch is the rent: every line rides every session forever, at the window's privileged start. So this act is about earning that position: rules that change behaviour, stated so a machine can follow them, and nothing else.

  • M5 teaches you to tell a rule that works from a rule that's decoration.
  • Hands-on 02 has you author the gym repo's CLAUDE.md and prove one rule with an A/B.
M5 · Rules that get followed

A rule earns its place or it poisons the file

GoalCalibrate your editor's eye on six candidate rules before you write your own.

A CLAUDE.md fails more often by bloat than by absence.

  • Aspirational prose ("write clean code") spends attention and changes nothing.
  • Stale rules are worse: one instruction that contradicts the code teaches the model to distrust the whole file.
  • A rule that works has three parts. The trigger says when it applies, the imperative says do this or never that, and the check is a way to tell it was followed.
  • "Run node --test before claiming done" has all three parts. "Prioritise quality" has none.

Below are six rules "collected from the team" for the gym repo. Judge each one: does it clear the trigger-imperative-check bar, or is it rent without power?

Widget · the rule linter

How industry does it · Anthropic's own guidance is "short"

Anthropic's Claude Code best-practices guidance says to keep CLAUDE.md concise and human-readable. Document the commands, the style points that differ from defaults, and the things the model gets wrong without help. Refine it like any prompt, rather than letting it grow into a wiki.

Treat the file as an onboarding doc for a very fast, very literal new hire, and you get the behaviour. Treat it as a policy archive, and you just pay the cost.

Under the hood · @imports: depth on demand from the standing layer

CLAUDE.md supports imports: a line like @docs/testing.md pulls that file into the standing context too. Used well, it keeps the spine file short while wiring in depth that genuinely belongs everywhere.

Used badly, it's how a 30-line file secretly becomes a 3,000-token one. Everything imported rides every session, exactly as if you had pasted it. Price an import like a rule. Does the whole file earn always-on placement? Or should Claude just read it when the task touches that area?

What you decide: your cut line. When in doubt, cut: a lean file that's always obeyed beats a complete one that's half-trusted.

Act 3 · Days 2–3 · ~50 min

Scoping: every fact has one correct address

You now have two files that work: your global CLAUDE.md from week 1's reinforcement, and the gym's from Act 2. Multiply that by every repo, package and workspace you touch, and the real question of this discipline appears: where does each rule live?

File it too high, and it pollutes every session on your machine. File it too low, and it starves the sessions that needed it. The scoping doctrine gives every fact one address at one of five levels: global, workspace, project, directory, local. Wrong scope is the quiet failure mode; nothing errors, everything slowly degrades.

  • M7 shows how the five scopes load, mechanically.
  • M8 has you classify eight real artifacts.
  • Hands-on 03 builds the monorepo and microservices cases on the gym variants.
  • Hands-on 04 makes it all agent-portable with one symlink.
In plain words · the five scopes in one breath
  • Global (~/.claude/) is you, everywhere: your style and your reinforcement paragraphs.
  • Workspace (a parent folder above several repos) holds facts that span sibling repos.
  • Project (repo root, checked in) holds this codebase's rules, shared with the team.
  • Directory (a CLAUDE.md inside a subfolder) holds one package's local conventions.
  • Local (gitignored files like CLAUDE.local.md) holds your personal overrides for one repo, never shared.

Five addresses exist, and every fact gets exactly one.

M7 · Five scopes, one walk

The tool walks up the tree. That's the whole mechanism.

GoalPredict, for any launch directory, exactly which files load: before the widget tells you.

At session start, Claude Code assembles the standing context the same way every time.

  • Your global file loads first.
  • Next it collects every CLAUDE.md it finds walking up the directory tree from where you launched; the current folder's own file loads last.
  • Folders below you load lazily: when Claude works on files inside a subdirectory, that subdirectory's CLAUDE.md joins the window on demand.

That's the whole mechanism. The scoping doctrine is applied knowledge of one walk up and one lazy load down.

Both trees below are this week's gym variants, exactly as you copied them.

  • Choose an architecture and a launch directory: the folder you run claude from, your cwd.
  • Teal rows load at launch; amber rows load on demand later.
  • The right-hand list shows the assembled standing context in load order, with each file's scope named.
Widget · the tree walker

Under the hood · what "loads" means, and where local fits

All loaded files are concatenated into the standing context; nothing "overrides" anything mechanically. If root says tabs and a package file says spaces, the model holds both and wobbles. So the doctrine is deduplication: each fact lives at exactly one scope, and the more specific file carries only what's genuinely local to it.

The fifth scope, local, is for facts that are yours alone on this machine: a gitignored CLAUDE.local.md next to the shared file. Sandbox URLs, personal ports and half-finished experiments go there. The shared files stay clean, and your machine still knows.

By the way · this is why you copied the variants to ~

If relay-mono still sat inside your roadmap repo clone, the roadmap's own CLAUDE.md would be a parent on the walk. It would leak into every drill session. That is the doctrine's first real lesson rather than a trick invented for the deck. You are always standing somewhere in a tree, and everything above you is in the room.

GoalMake both gym variants agent-portable, and demonstrate the drift-proof property rather than assuming it.

Your repos will outlive your tooling choices. AGENTS.md is the emerging cross-agent convention. Codex-class tools, Cursor and a growing list of others look for it, the way Claude Code looks for CLAUDE.md.

The wrong move is maintaining two files that drift apart. The right move is one source of truth and a symlink: a filesystem alias that makes AGENTS.md and CLAUDE.md the same bytes forever. It costs thirty seconds per repo, and your context layer stops being tool-specific.

terminal

terminal
The tail shows your new line through the AGENTS.md name; readlink shows the alias pointing home. One edit, visible under both names: drift is now impossible rather than merely discouraged. Remove the check line afterwards.

Git shows the link as a special file? Correct and fine: git stores symlinks first-class, and collaborators get the link on checkout (on Windows clones, per the note above). Commit it like any file.

Act 4 · Day 4 · ~40 min

The documentation layer: context that waits its turn

CLAUDE.md rides every session, so it must stay lean. But a repo knows more than rules: what the system is, how it's shaped, what must never happen, why it's built this way.

That knowledge belongs in a documentation layer. That is a small set of files written for a reader with no memory and no context. They sit on disk until a session actually needs them. Claude reaches them the same way it reaches code: by reading files mid-task.

The layer is cheap when unused and decisive when used: the opposite trade to the always-on spec. The two designs work as a pair.

  • M11 covers how to write docs an agent can actually use, and which doc answers which question.
  • M12 covers ADRs, the memory that survives every /clear.
  • Hands-on 05 has you author the layer for the workspace, and catch it changing a destructive decision.
M11 · Docs written for agents

Write for a brilliant reader with total amnesia

GoalRoute real questions to the right file instinctively: the skill that makes the layer worth writing.

Docs for agents obey harsher rules than docs for humans, because the reader arrives mid-task with zero shared history and reads selectively. Three habits follow.

  • Answer-first headings: a heading is a question someone will grep for, and its first sentence is the answer.
  • Self-contained sections: no "as discussed above"; any section may be the only one read.
  • One home per fact: four small single-purpose files beat one wiki, because the reader routes by filename before reading a word.

This programme standardises four files, with CLAUDE.md keeping only the always-on rules.

  • CONTEXT.md says what the system is, for a cold reader: domain, shape, vocabulary.
  • DESIGN.md says how it's built now: modules, ownership, data flow.
  • guardrails.md is the never-do list.
  • ADRs (architecture decision records, one small file per decision, in docs/adr/) say why it's built this way.

Every question below is one a fresh session genuinely asks about the relay workspace, silently, in its first minutes. Route each to the file that should answer it.

Widget · the cold-session router

How industry does it · this layer is convergent, not invented here

Agent-facing repo docs are converging fast across the industry: a what-is file for cold starts, a current-architecture file, an explicit constraints file, and per-decision records. Names vary by team, and the split does not.

The split mirrors how agents fail. They get the mental model wrong (CONTEXT), guess ownership wrong (DESIGN), take a forbidden action (guardrails), or relitigate a decision (ADR). You'll meet the same split, differently dressed, in week 8's named methodologies.

By the way · how sessions find these files at all

There are two routes. The first is agentic search. The filenames are self-describing, so a gathering agent lists the repo and reads the obvious candidate. The second is the pointer. One CLAUDE.md line, such as "Before changing data files or shared schemas, read guardrails.md", buys routing for a dozen tokens of always-on cost. Keep the pointer in the spine file and the weight on the disk.

What you decide: the three questions your real repo gets asked most. Friday you write the files that answer them; everything else can wait.
M12 · ADRs: decision memory

ADRs: the only memory that survives /clear

GoalRead one real ADR the way an agent does: as a constraint with a reason attached.

Every session you'll ever run forgets everything at the end. Your team's decisions can't afford to.

  • An architecture decision record is one small file, written once at decision time: the context, the decision, the consequences.
  • It costs ten minutes when the reasons are fresh, then pays out for years. Every future session, and every future teammate, can read why. Nobody has to guess, relitigate, or quietly undo it.
  • This week you read and obey ADRs. Week 4 drills writing them taught in week 4. Week 5's review harness checks changes against them taught in week 5.

This file ships in the workspace variant at relay-api/docs/adr/0001-append-only-archive.md, and it's load-bearing for Hands-on 05:

ADR 0001Decision: the archive array is append-only; no process may delete, rewrite or dedupe entries in the file. Ids increase monotonically and are never reused. Context: finance export and standup bot key on ids; a reused id once double-billed a client; "cleanup" scripts have silently lost rows twice. Consequences: duplicates are expected; consumers dedupe at read time; bad rows are fixed by appending corrections.

Read it the way an agent does.

  • The decision is a hard constraint on any edit it plans.
  • The context is why the "obvious cleanup" is an incident replay.
  • The consequences hand it the compliant alternative: dedupe at read time, which relay-report already does.

One small file converts a plausible destructive edit into an informed refusal, with no human in the loop. The table shows where each file earns its keep, by lifecycle:

FileEnters the windowWrittenGoes stale?
CLAUDE.mdEvery session, automaticallyAct 2; pruned continuouslyFastest: audit it like code
CONTEXT.mdCold starts, on demandOnce; touched rarelySlowly: domain shifts slowly
DESIGN.mdArchitecture questions, on demandNow; kept honest as things moveThe drift risk: week 4's habit taught in week 4
guardrails.mdBefore risky operations, on demandFrom incidents + ADR decisionsRarely: never-dos age well

Did you know · ADRs predate agents by over a decade

Michael Nygard's 2011 blog post "Documenting Architecture Decisions" popularised the format, and teams adopted it for entirely human reasons: onboarding, audits, ending relitigation. Agents didn't create the need. They raised the price of ignoring it. An agent relitigates a decision at machine speed, unless the record is on disk where it gathers.

M14 · Clear, compact, hand off

A heavy session has three exits, and choosing between them is the skill

GoalMake the right call at three real junctures, against the clock of a filling window.
  • /clear wipes the conversation: you get an instant lean window, and everything unwritten is gone.
  • /compact summarises the conversation and restarts on the summary: the window shrinks, the gist survives, and the verbatim detail dies. You can steer what survives with an instruction, like /compact keep the decisions and the failing tests.
  • The handoff ends the session entirely and leaves an authored file for the next one.

Wait too long and the choice is made for you. Near the limit, auto-compact fires at a moment it picks. It summarises whatever happens to be there, junk included.

The timeline below is a realistic day on a real repo. Grey rows are events; at each tinted juncture, pick the move you'd make. Verdicts argue from what the window holds at that moment: that's always the argument, never habit or superstition.

Widget · the session timeline

Under the hood · what /compact keeps, mechanically

Compact asks the model to write a structured summary of the conversation so far. It then starts a fresh window seeded with that summary. Your standing layers reload as always. Anything not in the summary is gone: exact code hunks, precise error text, the verbatim wording of an instruction.

That's why the focus argument matters. /compact keep the decisions, open bugs and file list makes the keep-list explicit. It stops a summariser doing the triage mid-squeeze. And it's why a rule that must survive compaction belongs in CLAUDE.md, which never depends on the summary at all.

M15 · Search beats pasting

Stop feeding it files. It can find them better itself.

GoalRetire paste-by-default; keep paste-on-purpose for the cases it genuinely wins.

Three verbs move text into the window.

  • Paste means you choose the text, and it stays in the window forever.
  • @-ref means typing @ and a path in your prompt; Claude Code injects that file's current bytes. It's precise, current, and free of copy drift.
  • Search means you name the goal, and the agent greps, lists and reads only what it needs. That is the gather beat from week 1, doing context selection for you.

Chat habits made pasting your default. In an agent session it's usually the worst of the three. You pay for your guess of what's relevant, at full window prices, whether you guessed right or not. Four situations from your actual week are below; pick the verb for each.

In the chat window, pasting was the only way to give the model eyes: no tools, so you were the file system. That reflex was correct for years and is obsolete in an agent session, where the model has grep and read of its own. M16 · The handoff

The handoff: authored context beats residue

GoalKnow the five sections and the quality test: could a stranger's session continue from this file alone?

Why not just /compact at 6pm and keep the session overnight? Because compact keeps what a summariser guessed mattered, and tomorrow-you inherits residue with no say in what survived.

A handoff flips the authorship: before ending, the session writes a small file that you review and correct while you still know everything. Tomorrow's session starts lean, reads one authored page, and continues as if the day boundary weren't there. It's the week's thesis applied to time: decide what the model sees, even tomorrow's model.

The template ships at weeks/week-2/data/handoff-template.md; here is what each section is for, and the failure it prevents:

SectionHoldsPrevents
StateDone and verified · half-done · untouched, one line eachRe-verifying finished work; trusting unfinished work
DecisionsChoices made this session, each with its one-line reasonTomorrow relitigating today (the ADR failure, at day scale)
Next stepThe single next action, cold-startable: file, command, intentThe 25-minute "where was I" ramp
GotchasThe trap that already cost you time once todayPaying for the same discovery twice
VerifyCommands that prove the state still holdsBuilding on a base that shifted overnight

The quality bar is transferability. Ask: could a session with no memory of today continue from this page alone? Every session is that stranger. And note the boundary with Act 4: a decision that should outlive the week graduates from the handoff's Decisions section into an ADR. The handoff holds the working set; the ADRs hold the archive.

By the way · handoffs compound beyond day boundaries

The same file works whenever a window must die mid-stream. That covers a risky /clear, switching machines, and a session that has degraded past compacting. It also covers handing work to an actual colleague and their session.

Once the habit exists, "this session is past saving" stops being a loss. Two minutes of handoff, and the work continues elsewhere, clean.

What you decide: your 6pm ritual. The programme's: handoff written by the session, corrected by you, committed if the repo is yours: then /clear or close without guilt.
