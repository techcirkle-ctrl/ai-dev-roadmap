In plain words · what "spec-driven" means here

Picture a builder and an extension on your house. You could stand in the garden describing it while they lay bricks. They would build fast, and you would correct them daily.

Or you agree the drawings first: where the door goes, where the wiring runs, what happens to the drain. Then the builder works for a week without needing you.

Spec-driven development is the drawings. You write down what the change is, what it must not break, and how you will know it worked. The drawings cost an hour. The corrections they prevent cost days.

What is · your last feature

You made every decision inside the diff, one surprise at a time

Look at what actually happened on the last feature you built with Claude.

  • You started from a sentence like "add rate limiting to the relay API", which hid at least four decisions you had not made.
  • Eleven minutes in, the session asked whether the limit was per team or per key. You answered in the chat, and that answer now lives only in a transcript.
  • Forty minutes in, you realised the counter had to survive a restart, which cost you the afternoon.
  • A reviewer asked why you chose that store, so you wrote three paragraphs in a PR comment. Nobody looks in PR comments six months later.
  • Two weeks later a teammate hit the same question and made the opposite call.

Every one of those is a decision made at the most expensive moment. Deciding while code exists means the code has to change. Deciding in a chat message means the reason evaporates.

Act 1 · Day 1 · ~40 min

Two controls you already have, and rarely use

Act 1 covers two controls that ship with Claude Code. One decides how hard it reasons before answering. The other decides whether it may change your files yet. Most people leave both on their defaults for months.

Together they are what makes planning possible. Without them you get an eager assistant that edits while you are still thinking. With them you get a colleague who reads, argues, and touches nothing until you agree.

  • M1 teaches when a task deserves longer reasoning, and corrects a common belief about how to ask for it.
  • M2 shows exactly what plan mode blocks and what it still allows.
  • Hands-on 01 runs one real task twice, straight through and then planned.
In plain words · "thinking" and "permission mode" here

Thinking is the working out Claude does before it writes its answer. It is real work and takes real time, the way a colleague pausing before answering takes time.

A permission mode is the standing answer to one question: what may this session do without asking me? It is a setting on the session, not an instruction in your prompt, which is why the model cannot talk its way past it.

M1 · When to think longer

Longer reasoning pays on decisions, and wastes your time on lookups

GoalCall eight real tasks correctly: just go, reason deeper, or plan first. Learn the one phrase that actually raises reasoning.

There are three separate ways reasoning gets raised, and they work at different levels.

  • The session toggle: Option+T on a Mac, or Alt+T on Windows and Linux, switches thinking on and off for the current session.
  • Your standing default: run /config and set thinking there, so every new session starts the way you want it.
  • One recognised keyword: put ultrathink anywhere in a prompt and reasoning rises for that turn only, leaving your session setting alone.

Phrases like "think hard" or "think step by step" are not recognised keywords. Claude Code passes them through as ordinary prompt text.

Deeper reasoning pays when a task has several defensible answers and picking wrong is expensive. It wastes time on lookups, renames, and anything with one correct result.

Under the hood · effort levels, and why invented phrases do nothing

On current models, reasoning is governed by an effort level rather than an on-off switch. Higher effort means more working out, which costs time and usage allowance.

The ultrathink keyword adds an instruction into the context for that one turn, which is why it leaves your session setting alone.

That also explains why invented phrases do nothing. No hidden parser looks for synonyms. A phrase is either a keyword the tool recognises or just more text in your prompt.

By the way · verify both controls in thirty seconds

Open a session and press Option+T, or Alt+T on Windows and Linux, and watch what the interface tells you. Then run /config and find the thinking setting.

Do both once and you will never again wonder which one you are on. Verifying a tool claim yourself beats trusting a slide, and this is the cheapest check all week.

M2 · Plan mode is a locked door

Plan mode blocks edits, and still lets Claude read your whole repo

GoalBuild an exact model of plan mode by firing seven actions at it and seeing which get through.

Plan mode is often called read-only. That is wrong in a way that matters.

  • Reading is fully allowed. A plan written without reading the code is a guess.
  • Exploring is allowed. Searching, listing files and checking git history still run, though your setup decides whether they stop to ask you.
  • Edits are blocked until you approve the plan. That single guarantee is the whole feature.

Press Shift+Tab to cycle the permission modes until you reach plan, or prefix one prompt with /plan. When the plan is ready, Claude stops and asks whether to proceed, let edits run, or keep planning.

Fire each action below at plan mode and watch what happens.

Widget · the plan mode gate · seven actions, two modes
Pick an action to fire it at the current mode.

Under the hood · why plan mode is a guarantee, not a request

Plan mode is a permission mode, and permission modes live in the tool rather than in the conversation. The check runs before a tool call, and the model gets no vote.

This is week 3's lesson again: text asks and code enforces. Plan mode is code.

The other modes sit on the same Shift+Tab cycle. Default asks before edits, accept edits stops asking, and plan blocks edits until you approve.

Act 2 · Day 2 · ~40 min

Four stages, and each one owns different questions

Act 2 has one idea. Work moves through four stages: brainstorm, spec, plan, then execute. Each stage owns a different kind of question. Ask one too early and you are guessing. Ask it too late and you are rewriting.

Most people already run all four stages. They run them jumbled together in one session, without naming any of them. Naming the stage you are in tells you which question you are allowed to answer right now.

  • M4 gives you the four stages and the question each one owns.
  • M5 shows what a decision costs when you find it late.
  • Hands-on 02 runs brainstorm alone on your vaguest ticket, with no code at all.
In plain words · "stage" and "rework" here

A stage is a period of work with one job. You may finish a stage while things are still uncertain, as long as the uncertainty belongs to a later stage.

Rework is work you did, undid, then did again differently. It is the most expensive thing in software and the least visible. Nobody logs it, and it looks like ordinary work while it is happening.

M4 · Four stages, four kinds of question

Every question belongs to exactly one stage, and answering it early is guessing

GoalPlace a question in its stage on sight, using ten real questions from one feature.

Here are the four stages and the job each one does.

  • Brainstorm decides what problem you are solving and what you are deliberately not solving. This stage may change your mind about the whole feature.
  • Spec decides what must be true of the finished change: behaviour, scope, constraints, and how you will know it worked. It never names files.
  • Plan decides the order of the work and which files it touches. It is the first stage that knows your codebase, and the last one that is cheap to throw away.
  • Execute writes the code and proves it works. A real question arriving here means something upstream was left undecided.

A stage is done when the only open questions belong to a later stage. Not when everything is certain, because everything never is.

Under the hood · why a spec ages slowly and a plan ages fast

Each stage needs a different amount of context. Brainstorm needs almost none, because it is about the problem. Spec needs the problem and the constraints, but no file paths.

Plan is the first stage that must read your repository, which is why it is version sensitive. A plan written against last month's code is often wrong today.

So the spec is the artifact you keep, and the plan is the artifact you regenerate.

M5 · What a late decision costs

The same decision costs about twenty times more in execute than in brainstorm

GoalMove one decision's discovery point across the four stages and watch what has to be thrown away at each.

Take one decision. Where does the rate limit counter live: in each process's memory, or in a shared store that survives restarts?

  • In brainstorm the cost is one sentence, because nothing exists yet.
  • In spec the cost is a paragraph, and the constraint now shapes everything downstream.
  • In plan the cost is a re-plan, which is annoying rather than expensive.
  • In execute the cost is the code, its tests, and the review time other people already spent. You pay for everything built on top of it.

The exact multiplier varies by team. What holds everywhere is the shape: cost rises with how much work already rests on the decision.

How industry does it · reviewing the design, not just the diff

Teams that take this seriously call it shifting left, because early stages sit on the left of a drawn timeline. It shows up as concrete habits.

Design documents get reviewed before implementation starts. Plans get approved before branches get cut. A question raised during execution is treated as a defect in the upstream artifact rather than as normal chatter.

That last habit is the one that changes behaviour. When an execution question means somebody must fix the spec, specs quietly get better.

In plain words · what "a cold session" means

A cold session is a fresh Claude Code session that has never seen your conversation. No memory of what you discussed, and no idea what you meant by the word export. It has your repository and your spec, and nothing else.

Think of handing a recipe to someone in another kitchen. You cannot shout across to them. Whatever the card does not say, they guess at or ring you about.

M7 · Six parts, six failures

Each missing section of a spec fails in its own recognisable way

GoalDiagnose a spec from its symptom, by switching off each section and reading the failure it causes.

A spec needs six parts. Each exists because leaving it out causes a specific failure.

  • Context: why the change exists and who is hurting. Missing, the session solves the wrong problem well.
  • Behaviour: what the system must do afterwards, in testable terms.
  • Scope: what is deliberately excluded. Skipped most often, regretted most often.
  • Constraints: what must not break, and what the change must live within.
  • Decisions: the calls already made and why. Missing, the session makes them differently.
  • Acceptance: how anyone can tell it worked. Missing, "done" becomes an opinion.

Nothing on that list names a file or an order of work. Both belong to the plan, and keeping them out is what lets a spec survive a refactor.

How industry does it · the same six parts under different names

Teams name these differently. Some write requirements documents with goals and non-goals. Some write design documents with a context section and an alternatives section. Some split requirements, design and tasks across three files.

The names vary and the content does not. Non-goals is scope wearing a different hat.

If you inherit a template at work, map it against these six rather than fighting it. Whatever it is missing is what your sessions will keep asking you about.

M8 · Drive the questions to zero

Patch the gap a question points at, and three other questions disappear with it

GoalRun the week's core loop: read the questions, patch the section each one points at, watch the count fall.

Here is the loop. Hand the spec over cold, collect the questions, patch the spec, hand it over cold again.

Never answer a question in the chat. Answer it in the spec and re-run. Answering in chat fixes this session and leaves the spec as broken as it was.

  • Questions cluster. Four questions usually point at one missing section, not four holes.
  • Some questions belong elsewhere. "Which test runner?" is week 2's documentation layer, not something every spec repeats.
  • Watch for "I'll assume". A silent decision is more dangerous than a question.

Under the hood · why the count falls in steps, not smoothly

A missing section does not cause one gap. It causes every question that depends on it.

Leave scope out and the session must ask about each thing it might reasonably include, which is three or four questions on its own.

So patching feels disproportionately effective. You are removing the reason a family of questions existed, which is why the count sits still and then drops several at once.

Act 4 · Day 4 · ~45 min

Write the reason down once, at the moment you have it

Code records what you chose. It never records what you rejected, or why. Six months later that missing half is the expensive one, because the next person re-argues a question you already settled.

Two documents carry that memory. An ADR captures one decision at the time it was made and never changes. DESIGN.md describes the system as it is now, and changes whenever the system does.

  • M10 teaches which decisions earn a record, because recording everything is as useless as recording nothing.
  • M11 shows how a design document goes stale, and how to catch it.
  • Hands-on 04 writes one real record for a decision you already made.
In plain words · what an ADR actually is

An architecture decision record is one short file. It says what you decided, what else you considered, why you chose this one, and what changing your mind would cost. Most good ones fit on a page.

Think of it as the note you leave for whoever inherits this code. Not how the code works, because the code shows that. The crossroads you stood at, and why you went left.

Teams number them and keep them together, so they read as a history rather than a pile.

M10 · Which decisions earn a record

A decision earns a record when reversing it is expensive and the reason is not obvious

GoalApply a two-question threshold to eight real decisions, fast enough to use it while working.

Two questions settle almost every case, asked in this order.

  • What does reversing this cost? A rename is cheap. A migration, a coordinated deploy or a customer conversation is not.
  • Would a competent colleague guess your reason from the code? If yes, the code is the record. If they would guess wrong, write it down.

Both must say yes. Expensive with an obvious reason needs nothing. Cheap with a strange reason needs a code comment. Only expensive and non-obvious earns a file.

If you rejected something a reasonable person would have chosen, write the record anyway. The value is stopping someone re-discovering why the obvious answer fails here.

How industry does it · Nygard's format, and why they live in the repo

The practice comes from a short 2011 article by Michael Nygard, worth five minutes of your time. His format has barely changed since: title, status, context, decision, consequences.

Teams keep them numbered in a folder inside the repo, usually docs/adr/. That matters more than the format. A record in a wiki drifts away from the code. A record beside the code moves with it and gets reviewed with it.

Week 5 puts this to work: your review harness checks changes against these records, which only works because they are files in the repo.

M11 · Catching a stale design doc

A design document goes wrong quietly, one merged commit at a time

GoalRead a design document against four commits that landed after it, and find the line that stopped being true.

Week 2 had you write DESIGN.md. Here is what happens to it. Each commit changes the system a little, and no single commit feels like it invalidates a document.

  • Stale is worse than absent. Missing information makes people look. Wrong information makes them act confidently on something untrue.
  • Your sessions read it too. A wrong line becomes a wrong assumption in every session that starts.
  • The fix is a habit. A change that makes a line untrue updates that line in the same pull request.

There is a cheap audit for when the habit slips. Give a session the file and the last month of commits, and ask only for contradictions.

By the way · one file is immutable, the other is not

A decision record is immutable. When a decision is reversed you do not edit the old record. You write a new one that supersedes it and mark the old one superseded, so the history survives.

A design document is the opposite. It describes the present, so you edit it freely and let git carry the history.

One file remembers why. The other describes what. Mixing them gives you a design document full of archaeology, which nobody reads.

Pick a decision already made in your repo, ideally from the last three months. Choose one where you rejected something reasonable.

Writing about the past has a trap. You know how it turned out, so the record sounds inevitable. Write what you knew at the time, including what you were unsure about.

Step 1 — gather what actually happened
Find the pull request, commit or thread where it landed. Skim for what you considered and rejected, because that is the part people never write down.
Step 2 — draft it from the template
Copy weeks/week-4/data/adr-template.md into docs/adr/ and number it. Keep it under a page. Longer usually means you are explaining how the code works rather than why you chose it.
Step 3 — test it cold, then on a human
Read docs/adr/<file>.md. You were not part of this decision.

1. What would you still need to ask before you could defend this choice to
   someone who disagrees with it?
2. Which alternative would a reasonable engineer have picked instead, and does
   the record explain why it was rejected?
3. Does the record say what it would cost to reverse this? Quote the line, or
   say it is missing.
claude
Patch the record, then send it to the teammate who lived through the decision. Ask only this: does this match what we actually knew at the time?
  • At least one rejected option is named, with the reason it lost
  • The cost of reversing it is stated in concrete terms
  • It is committed in the repo, not in a wiki
  • It reads as what you knew then, not as a story with an obvious ending
Stuck? · no memory of alternatives, it keeps growing, your teammate disagrees

Cannot remember the alternatives? Read the pull request comments rather than the code. Reviewers usually name the option you rejected.

It keeps growing? You are describing the implementation. Cut every sentence about how it works and keep the ones about why.

Your teammate disagrees? That is the most useful thing you will get all week. Record both readings rather than picking a winner.

Check your read: a stranger should read it and either stop arguing or argue better. If they would still ask "why not just use the obvious thing", the rejected options are too thin. What you decide: name a concrete trigger for writing the next one.
Act 5 · Day 5 · ~35 min

Review the plan, while changing it still costs four minutes

You already review pull requests. By then the code exists, the tests exist, and someone has spent a day on it. Reviewing the plan catches the same mistakes when the fix is a paragraph.

Accepting a plan is one keystroke, and a keystroke is easy to press without reading. A written checklist turns it back into a decision.

  • M13 shows one mistake caught at four different points, and what each costs.
  • M14 gives you six checks that make a plan review concrete.
  • Hands-on 05 reviews a real plan against those checks, before code exists.
In plain words · what reviewing a plan actually involves

Reviewing a plan does not mean reading it and nodding. It means hunting for specific problems with a list, the same way you review code.

What you hunt for differs. In code review you hunt for bugs. In plan review you hunt for the wrong approach, the missed dependency, and the step that will turn out to be three.

M13 · Four places to catch it

One wrong approach costs four minutes in the plan and two days after release

GoalTake one real mistake to four different catch points and read what each one costs.

The plan proposes changing a shared middleware to add rate limiting. Three other services depend on it, and the plan does not say so.

  • In plan review the fix is one sentence: add a new middleware instead of changing the shared one.
  • In code review the fix is a rewrite plus its tests, after a day already spent.
  • In staging you lose the coordination too. Three teams need telling, and two have other work in flight.
  • In production you add an incident, a rollback and a conversation nobody planned for. The engineering is the smallest part.

The mistake is identical in all four rows. Only the work resting on it changes. Plan review is how you deliberately move the catch point left.

M14 · The six-check rubric

Six questions find nearly every plan failure, and each one has a shape you can spot

GoalFind the flaw in six real plans, each failing exactly one of the six checks.

Here are the six checks, in the order to run them.

  • Does it match the spec? A plan solving a slightly different problem survives review most often, because everything in it looks competent.
  • Does it touch anything shared? Other services, teams or repositories must be named explicitly.
  • Is every step really one step? "Update the API layer" is hiding three steps and a decision.
  • Does it say how you will know it worked? A plan with no verification ends in an opinion.
  • Does it contradict a recorded decision? Your ADRs start earning their keep here.
  • What is the rollback? If the answer is "revert the commit", check whether that is still true once a migration has run.

Find the failing check in each plan below before revealing it.

How industry does it · the rubric becomes a command

This is a good candidate for week 3's harness. The rubric becomes a slash command, so reviewing a plan is one word rather than six remembered questions.

Some teams add a hook so that accepting a plan without running the review is not possible.

That is the rule of twice applied here. You have now read these checks twice, which by that rule means they belong in a file.

Capstone · Step 5 of 5 · ~20 min

Log where reality left the plan, bring DESIGN.md back in line, then ship it

GoalA divergence log, an honest DESIGN.md, a raised pull request, and the report assembled for your Knowledge issue.

Compare what you planned against what happened. Every difference goes in the log with its cause, because the cause is the useful part.

  • Spec was wrong: usually a constraint you only discovered by building.
  • Plan was wrong: usually a step that turned out to be three.
  • The world changed: someone else merged while you worked. Nobody's fault, still worth counting.
  • You changed your mind: watch these. A mind changed mid-execution is usually a decision that belonged three stages earlier.

Before you raise anything, read DESIGN.md against what you just built. Any line the feature made untrue gets fixed in this same pull request. That is Act 4's habit, and doing it here is what stops the file drifting.

Then raise the pull request, linking the spec, the plan and the ADR from its description.
